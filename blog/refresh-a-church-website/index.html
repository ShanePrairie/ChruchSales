How to Refresh a Stale Church Website Without Starting Over

Most stale church websites need a disciplined edit of facts and paths before they need a brand-new build.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

Most stale church websites need a disciplined edit of facts and paths before they need a brand-new build.

List what is wrong before you pick a new look

A site feels stale for specific reasons: the service time is wrong, the pastor photo is from two leaders ago, the events page is a graveyard, the menu uses names nobody says, or the homepage is a video that never states the address. Write those problems down. A new color will not fix a wrong time. A rebuild that copies the old content forward will feel new for a week.

Start a simple audit of the pages guests use. The design checklist is a ready list of tasks. Mark each page keep, edit, or retire. Retiring an unused page is a refresh. You do not owe the internet a permanent copy of every announcement from a decade ago.

  • Correct every service time, address, and staff role.
  • Remove or replace photos you no longer have permission to use.
  • Fix or delete broken links and empty forms.
  • Rewrite the homepage's first screen in plain facts.
  • Check the site on a phone off the office network.

Fix the visit path before the interior pages

If a newcomer cannot tell when and where to come, nothing else on the site is the priority. Edit the first screen and the plan-your-visit page in the same week so they match. Then align the map listing. Interior pages about history can wait.

While you are there, remove popups and sliders that hide the time. A refresh that adds motion and removes facts is a step backward. The speed guide will tell you if the new look is also heavier than the old one.

Keep what the team will actually maintain

A feature you cannot update is not an asset. If nobody will post sermons with titles, do not promise a library. If nobody will edit a blog, do not leave twelve draft buttons in the menu. Choose a smaller site the office can keep true. Write the maintenance tasks next to jobs that already exist, such as the weekly bulletin.

Ask who has the passwords and whether a volunteer could lose them. A refresh includes access. Two people should be able to change a service time. Document the steps in a shared note, not in one person's memory.

While you edit, keep a list of URLs that other people already use: the bulletin, the email signature, the map listing, and last year's invite. Change the page at that address rather than creating a new one and abandoning the old one. If you must move a page, redirect the old address. A refresh that breaks every saved link feels like the site disappeared.

Keep a short changelog for the team: the page, the date, and the fact you corrected. You do not need to publish it. It stops the next editor from undoing a time change because an old bulletin is still on their desk.

Replace content in passes, not in a single heroic weekend

Pass one is facts: times, places, names, and links. Pass two is the guest path: homepage, visit, events, kids. Pass three is the pages members use: sermons, groups, giving, volunteer. Trying to rewrite the history page while the service time is wrong spends the only weekend people offered.

At the end of each pass, have someone who did not do the editing complete a task on a phone. If they fail, the pass is not done. Do not announce a relaunch until the guest path is true. A quiet improvement is better than a launch party for a site that still lists last year's Easter.

Know when a refresh is not enough

A new build is reasonable when you cannot edit the current site, when it is unsafe or inaccessible in ways you cannot patch, or when the platform will not let you publish a simple visit page. Even then, bring the edited words with you. A rebuild without a content pass repeats the staleness in a new template.

If you are unsure which side of that line you are on, describe what you cannot change and what guests complain about. Often the next step is a smaller edit. Sometimes it is a new site. The facts you gathered in the audit are what make either choice honest.

Frequently asked questions

Should we hide the site until the refresh is done?

Not if people rely on it for Sunday. Fix the time and the address in public first. Hide only pages that are so wrong they mislead.

How often should we do this audit?

A short pass every quarter, and a fuller look once a year. The calendar matters more than the size of the project.
