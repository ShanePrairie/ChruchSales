Church Homepage Copy: What to Say Above the Fold

The first screen should tell a guest who you are, when you gather, where you are, and how to take the next step.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry5 min read

The first screen should tell a guest who you are, when you gather, where you are, and how to take the next step.

Write for the phone, not the desktop mockup

People rarely meet a church homepage the way a committee reviews it on a laptop. They open a link from a map listing, a search result, or a text from a friend, often while deciding whether a Sunday visit is realistic. Above the fold means the part of the page visible before any scrolling. On a phone that space is short. A full-bleed photo, a rotating slogan, and a menu icon can fill it without stating a single fact a guest can use.

Treat that first screen as a spoken introduction. A newcomer should be able to read the church name, the town or neighborhood, the main gathering time, and the one action you hope they will take. If any of those four are missing, the headline is not finished, even if the design looks polished. Put the facts in text, not only inside an image, so they can be read, translated, and found by search.

  • Name the church in the first screen, not only in the logo.
  • State the usual day and time in words a stranger can repeat.
  • Name the neighborhood or town, not a room nickname.
  • Offer one obvious next step, such as planning a visit.

Lead with a fact, then a welcome

A warm line can sit beside a factual line, but it should not replace it. A sentence like a receptionist would say out loud is a good test: who gathers, where, and when. A slogan that could belong to any congregation does not help someone choose a drive across town. If you love a short welcome phrase, keep it, and put the service pattern directly underneath in ordinary type.

Skip the rotating banner that hides the one detail a guest needed. Sliders move the time off screen before a person can copy it into a calendar. A static block the office can edit when the pattern changes is easier to trust. If there are two services or a second site, say both in text. Do not make people wait for the third slide to learn that the gathering they wanted is at the other campus.

Make the button describe the next page

The main button should name the action. Plan your visit, see service times, or ask a question tells a person what will happen. Learn more does not. Send the button to a real page, not back to the top of the homepage. If that page asks for a name and email, say why in the button's destination, and say whether someone will reply. A button that promises a personal welcome and then only adds people to a general newsletter feels careless.

Keep a quieter second path for people who are not ready to attend. A text link to what the church believes, the sermon archive, or the page for parents is enough. Do not turn the first screen into a directory of every ministry. The menu can carry that list. The first screen carries the visit. When you are ready for another pair of eyes on the wording, tell the team what the page needs to explain.

Answer the questions guests actually ask

Under the headline, answer the questions people text a friend before they come. What time should I arrive? Is there anything for kids? What do people wear? Where do I park? One honest line each is more useful than a mission statement that could be copied onto another church's site. If the answer changes by week, link to the page the team already updates, such as the events page or the plan-your-visit guide, instead of pasting a detail that will go stale on the homepage.

Read every line out loud. If a sentence only makes sense to someone who already attends, rewrite it. Room nicknames, sermon-series brands, and ministry acronyms can live on interior pages where a member is looking for them. The homepage is for the person who does not know those names yet. The website checklist is a useful companion once the first screen is clear, because design and copy fail together when the facts are hard to see.

Edit on a phone, then ask a newcomer

Open the draft on a phone the church actually uses. Check that the name, the time, the place, and the button are visible together, or that the missing piece appears after one small movement rather than after a full-screen video. Turn off autoplay motion while you read. A moving background makes the sentence you worked on harder to finish. If a photo is the hero, make sure the text still has enough contrast to read in sunlight.

Then hand the phone to someone who does not maintain the site. Give them half a minute and ask for the service time and how they would visit. If they cannot answer, change the sentence rather than adding another banner. After the homepage is clear, the first-week welcome plan is the right next system to prepare, so a visit that starts online does not end in silence on Monday.

Frequently asked questions

What if the church has more than one service?

List each regular time in text, and name the campus or room when that changes where people should go. Do not rely on a photo of a bulletin.

Should the pastor's name be above the fold?

Put it there only if a newcomer needs it to recognize who is preaching or to match a name they already searched. A longer introduction belongs on the about page.
