Church Website Accessibility: A Practical Checklist

A more accessible church website is a series of concrete checks, not a redesign slogan.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

A more accessible church website is a series of concrete checks, not a redesign slogan.

Start with the pages guests use first

Accessibility work gets abandoned when it is framed as a someday audit of every PDF from 2009. Start with the pages a first-time guest touches: home, plan your visit, events, beliefs, sermons, giving, and contact. If those pages can be read and operated by someone who zooms the text, uses a keyboard, or cannot see the photos, you have done the most hospitable part.

This guide is a practical checklist, not a legal opinion. Requirements vary, and a specialist review is appropriate before you claim conformance with a standard. You can still fix obvious barriers this month. Pair the work with the guest checklist so content and access improve together.

  • Text can be resized without the layout breaking.
  • Color is not the only way information is shown.
  • Every form control has a visible label.
  • Videos that carry the message have captions.
  • The menu can be used without a mouse.

Make type and contrast easy to read

Small gray text on a photograph fails in sunlight and for many readers at a desk. Put important facts, especially times and addresses, in solid text over a steady background. Check contrast rather than trusting a brand color that looked fine in a slide deck. Underline links in body copy so people do not have to guess which words can be tapped.

Use real headings in order. A large bold line that is not a heading is invisible to someone navigating by heading. Do not skip from a page title to a tiny subheading with no structure between them. Left-align paragraphs. Justified text and all-caps sentences are harder to read and are rarely necessary for a welcome.

Write alt text and captions for real content

If a photo carries meaning, describe it in alt text in a short sentence: the scene, not a keyword list. If a photo is purely decorative, give it empty alt text so screen readers skip it. Do not put the only copy of the service time inside a picture. The photography guide covers permission and dignity; this checklist covers whether the image is perceivable.

Sermons and testimonies that are posted as video need captions if you expect people to follow them without sound. Auto captions are a start only if a person corrects names and scripture references. A downloadable transcript helps people who need to search or read at their own pace. A video that autoplays with sound should be removed or silenced by default.

Fix forms, menus, and motion

Every field needs a label that stays visible, not only placeholder text that disappears while someone types. Errors should be described in words, not only by a red border. Do not impose a time limit on a prayer request or a visit form. If a form has a required field, mark it in text.

Menus, dialogs, and carousels cause the most trouble. A person should be able to open and close them with a keyboard, and motion should respect reduced-motion settings. Decorative animation that runs constantly makes it harder to read the welcome you wrote. The speed guide overlaps here: heavy motion and heavy files often fail the same people.

Give the checklist an owner

Pick one person to walk the priority pages with this list each quarter, and after any redesign. Include a phone test and a keyboard-only test. Invite someone who uses the site differently from the design team, and pay attention to where they get stuck. Write down the fix and the page, not a vague note to improve accessibility later. Note the browser you used and the date, so the next person does not repeat a check you already finished.

When you add a new tool, such as a chat widget, a giving embed, or a livestream player, test that tool the same week. Third-party frames often break keyboard focus or trap people in a dialog. If you cannot operate it without a mouse, do not put it on the visit page until there is a plain HTML alternative with the same information.

When a barrier needs a structural change, such as a menu that cannot be operated or a giving flow that traps keyboard users, describe the page and what fails. Do not publish a badge that claims more than you have checked. A quiet, accurate page is more trustworthy than a seal you cannot explain.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a separate accessibility statement?

A short page that names known limits and a contact email is useful. Keep it honest and update it when something changes.

Are social media images part of this?

If the image is the announcement, repeat the time and place in the post text. The website should still hold the canonical details.
