How to Write a Church Plan Your Visit Page

A dedicated visit page should answer arrival questions before a guest has to guess or send a nervous text.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

A dedicated visit page should answer arrival questions before a guest has to guess or send a nervous text.

Give the visit its own page

A homepage can invite someone. It usually cannot hold every detail of arrival without becoming a wall of exceptions. A plan-your-visit page is the place for the practical sequence: when to come, where to park, which door to use, what the first ten minutes feel like, and who to ask if something is unclear. Link that page from the main button on the homepage and from the top of the menu, using the same words in both places.

Write the page for a person who has never seen the building. Avoid phrases such as the usual door or the room by the kitchen. Name the street entrance, the side of the building, and the person or desk that offers a first hello. If your church uses a clear homepage sentence already, this page is where that sentence gets specific.

  • Service times and the time you suggest arriving.
  • Parking, including accessible spaces and what to do when the lot is full.
  • The entrance to use, and what a guest will see first.
  • How long a typical gathering lasts.
  • Who to contact the day before if plans change.

Describe the room without selling a personality

People want to know whether they will be asked to stand, speak, or be singled out. Say what usually happens in the first few minutes and whether guests are invited to introduce themselves. If the church does not put visitors on the spot, say that plainly. If there is a welcome desk, say where it is and that stopping there is optional. Pressure disguised as hospitality makes the page less useful.

Clothing is a common worry. Describe what people actually wear instead of saying come as you are and leaving the photo gallery in formal attire. If the room is loud, quiet, liturgical, or informal, use those ordinary words. A short paragraph beats a mood video that never states the time. Pair this page with the guest-focused website checklist so the rest of the site does not contradict it.

Answer parents before they have to ask

Families decide partly on whether their children will be safe and oriented. Explain the ages you can receive, how check-in works, what a parent does on a first Sunday, and whether children stay in the main gathering. Mention allergies, security stickers, or paging a parent only if those systems are real and current. Do not describe a nursery you cannot staff this month.

Say where a parent goes if a child is upset, and how to ask about accessibility for a child who needs a quieter space. Link to the children's ministry page rather than pasting a second full manual here. The visit page should be enough to get a family through the front door. The ministry page can hold curriculum, calendars, and volunteer needs.

Put accessibility in the arrival instructions

Accessibility is part of the visit, not a footnote. Note step-free entrances, elevator or ramp locations, accessible restrooms, large-print materials if you offer them, and whether the sound system has an assistive-listening option. If a feature is limited, say so and name a person who can help plan ahead. A surprise at the door is harder to solve than a sentence on the website.

Include a simple way to ask a question that is not a public comment form. An email address or a phone number answered by the office is enough. If you invite people to tell you they are coming, explain what the church will do with that note. A short form can help a host prepare, but it should be optional. The follow-up guide explains how to continue the welcome after Sunday without assuming permission to text.

Keep the page current and easy to scan

Review the page whenever the clock changes, a door is under construction, or children's ministry shifts rooms. Put the last-reviewed habit on the office calendar, next to the bulletin deadline. Outdated parking instructions do more harm than a short page. Remove seasonal exceptions when the season ends, or move them to the events page so Christmas Eve directions do not sit on the ordinary visit page in June.

Use headings that match the questions: Parking, Kids, What to expect, Accessibility, Ask a question. A guest scanning on a phone should find the heading without reading every paragraph. When the page is solid, start a conversation if you want help connecting it to the homepage, the menu, and a welcome path that does not over-collect information.

Frequently asked questions

Should guests have to register before they attend?

No. Offer a way to say they are coming if it helps the host team, and make the form optional. People should still be able to walk in.

Where should service times be repeated?

On the homepage, on this visit page, and on your map listing. If one of those is wrong, fix it the same day rather than hoping guests will find the correct page.
