Bilingual and Multilingual Church Websites

A second language on a church website helps only when real people can keep those pages as accurate as the first.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

A second language on a church website helps only when real people can keep those pages as accurate as the first.

Add a language you can actually maintain

A second language is a commitment, not a plugin. Offer it when a part of the congregation or the neighborhood uses that language and someone qualified can review the pages on a schedule. Machine translation of the entire site, left unchecked, will mistranslate names, times, and doctrinal terms. A wrong service time in a second language is worse than no page.

Start with the pages a guest needs: home, plan your visit, events, beliefs, and contact. The website checklist is a useful order of work even when you are writing in two languages. Leave the archive and old PDFs until a person can review them. The visit page is the highest priority because it contains the facts people travel on.

  • Name who reviews each language.
  • Limit the first set of pages to guest essentials.
  • Put service times in each language in text.
  • Show which gatherings happen in which language.
  • Date the review so stale translations are visible to the team.

Make the switch obvious and stable

Put a language switch where people can see it on a phone, using the language's own name, not a flag alone. Flags are not languages. Send people to the matching page, not always to the homepage, when a translation exists. If a page is not translated, say so in the language they were reading rather than dumping them into another language without explanation.

Keep menus parallel. If the English menu says visit, events, and sermons, the other language should offer the same jobs, even if some destinations are still untranslated. The navigation guide applies in every language: labels should be the words a guest would use, reviewed by a speaker of that language.

Use human review for anything time-sensitive

Times, addresses, children's ages, and safety instructions need a person. A translator who knows the church will catch a phrase that is grammatically fine and culturally wrong. Build that review into the same workflow as the bulletin. Do not publish a Christmas schedule in one language only if you invited the other language community to come.

Beliefs and baptism language deserve the same care. A sloppy doctrinal sentence is not a small typo. Link both languages to the same underlying decisions so the pages do not drift into two different churches. The accessibility checklist still applies: translated text must be real text, not a picture of a paragraph.

Do not trap people in the wrong experience

If one service is in English and another is in another language, say that on both homepages and on the visit page. Say whether interpretation is offered, and through what means, only if it is reliable that week. A promise of interpretation that is not staffed is a broken welcome.

Forms should be available in the language of the page that linked to them, or you should warn that the form is still in one language and offer a phone or email alternative. A guest should not have to submit a prayer request they cannot read.

Budget time for the unglamorous updates: a changed phone number, a moved classroom, a holiday schedule. Those lines are short and easy to forget in the second language. Put them on the same checklist as the first language. If a reviewer is a volunteer, agree on how many pages they can realistically check in a month and do not assign them the whole site out of enthusiasm.

When a phrase is sensitive, such as a word for baptism or for communion, agree on the term before you translate the rest of the page. Consistency across the visit page and the beliefs page matters more than a clever variation.

Grow the library slowly

After the essential pages are solid, add sermons or event listings in the second language only as fast as someone can title and describe them. A single well-kept set of pages beats a full site that rotted after launch week. Remove a language you can no longer support rather than leaving it wrong.

If you are deciding whether a second language belongs on the site this year, describe who gathers and who can review. The right scope might be five pages and a clear switch, not a translated copy of every historical post.

Frequently asked questions

Is a translate button enough?

A browser tool can help a reader in a pinch. It is not a substitute for pages your church has reviewed, especially for times and beliefs.

What if only one ministry is bilingual?

Say that specifically. Do not imply the whole Sunday gathering is bilingual if only the children's class or one service is.
