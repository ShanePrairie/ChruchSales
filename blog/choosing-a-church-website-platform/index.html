How to Choose a Church Website Platform

Pick a church website platform the team can edit, that you can leave, and that lets guests find Sunday on a phone.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

Pick a church website platform the team can edit, that you can leave, and that lets guests find Sunday on a phone.

Start from the jobs, not the demo

A platform demo is designed to look easy. Your decision is whether a specific person on your team can change a service time on a Saturday, publish an event, and replace a photo without filing a ticket. List those jobs before you watch a tour. If the tool cannot do them in the words your editor uses, the tour does not matter.

Bring the content audit from the refresh guide into the conversation. A new platform will not supply your times, your beliefs, or your photos. It only hosts them. The budget guide can help you decide what not to buy yet. This page will not quote prices or rank vendors.

  • Who will edit the site in a normal week?
  • Can they change a time without a developer?
  • Do you keep your domain and a copy of your content if you leave?
  • Can the visit page be fast and readable on a phone?
  • Which extras, such as giving or chat, are optional?

Ask about ownership and exit

You should be able to keep your domain name even if you change tools. You should be able to export pages, sermons, and form submissions in a format someone else can read. Ask what happens to email addresses you collected if you cancel. A platform that treats the congregation's content as a hostage is a poor fit, even if the templates are attractive.

Also ask who can be an administrator and how you remove a volunteer who should no longer have access. Shared logins are a common failure. Two named admins and a written handoff are a reasonable minimum.

Ask where the site is hosted and what happens if the original designer disappears. A platform your team can log into is different from a site that exists only on one freelancer's computer. Request the steps for changing the domain before you need them. Ordinary maintenance should not require the person who did the first build.

Test the guest path, not the feature list

During a trial, build only the homepage, the visit page, and one event. Open them on a phone. The speed habits apply on day one: if the template forces a huge slider and three scripts before the service time, you will be fighting the tool forever. Accessibility basics belong in the trial too. If you cannot create a labeled form or a keyboard-friendly menu, keep looking.

Ignore features you have no one to run. A built-in app, a member directory, or a course platform is not a gift if it will sit empty and confuse the menu. Optional is better than bundled when the bundle adds clutter to every page.

Check how sermons, giving, and email connect

Some churches want sermons, giving, and email inside one login. Others already have tools people know. A platform should link cleanly to the tools you will keep, without requiring you to move everything in the same month. Ask how a sermon page is titled and whether the text is real text. Ask whether giving opens a clear, identifiable page.

Email and text tools should not assume you will message everyone you import. Permission still sits with the church, not the software. If a platform's newsletter feature makes opt-out hard, that is a reason to pause.

Decide with the editors in the room

The people who will maintain the site should click through the trial, not only the people who will sign the agreement. Give them a task: change a time, add an event, and unpublish a page. If they cannot, the platform is not simple enough, no matter what the brochure says. Write down the reasons you chose, so the next committee does not relitigate from a sales call.

If you want a second opinion on a short list, tell us the jobs the site must do and who will edit it. We will not pretend one tool fits every congregation. The right platform is the one your team will still be updating next season.

Frequently asked questions

Should a small church use the same platform as a large one?

Not necessarily. A small team needs an editor that is obvious and a site that is easy to leave. Extra features are not a sign of maturity.

Can we switch later?

Yes, if you own the domain and can export the content. Choose with the exit in view so a later switch is a project, not a crisis.
