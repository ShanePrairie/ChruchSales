Church Website Navigation: Help Guests Find the Next Step

A good church menu is a short list of next steps, written in words a first-time guest would use.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

A good church menu is a short list of next steps, written in words a first-time guest would use.

Name pages the way a guest would search

Navigation fails when the labels belong to the org chart. A guest is not looking for a department called assimilation or a brand name that only members recognize. They are looking for visit, events, beliefs, sermons, kids, and contact. Use those words, or words very close to them, in the main menu. Creative ministry names can remain as page titles underneath a clear parent label.

Order the menu by the jobs a newcomer has, not by which ministry was founded first. Plan your visit and events usually belong near the start. Giving can be present without being the first item. Contact should be easy to find, not buried as the last tiny link in a footer only. The homepage copy guide covers what the first screen says; the menu is how people move after that.

  • Plan your visit
  • What we believe
  • Events
  • Sermons
  • Kids and students
  • Contact

Keep the top menu short

A top menu with more than about seven items becomes a list people do not read, especially on a phone where it turns into a tall stack. Group lesser-used pages under a clear parent instead of giving every ministry its own top-level link. About can hold leadership, beliefs, and a short history. Visit can hold times, parking, and what to expect if you do not want all of that in the top bar.

Do not duplicate the same destination under three labels. New here, plan a visit, and i'm new should not lead to three slightly different pages. Pick one label and one URL. Extra paths create extra pages to update when the service time changes. The design checklist is a good audit partner: if a task is important, it deserves one obvious path.

Design the phone menu as the real menu

Most guests will open the menu with a thumb. Make the tap targets tall enough to hit without zooming, and do not hide the visit link inside a second drawer. The first items in the open menu should match the first items on a desktop. A different order on mobile guarantees that someone on staff will test the wrong version and miss a broken link.

Include a visible close control and make sure the keyboard can move through the links. If a menu covers the page, a person using a screen reader should not be trapped behind it. Test with the actual Sunday announcement link, because that is the URL people tap in a hurry. A menu that only works when someone carefully hovers on a laptop is not a menu for guests.

Use the footer as a second map, not a junk drawer

People who scroll to the bottom are often looking for a practical fact: address, email, times, or a privacy note. Repeat the main destinations in the footer and add the pages that did not fit in the top menu, such as giving, jobs or volunteer interest, and a link back to the journal or resources if you publish them. Keep the address in text.

Do not paste every sermon series and every PDF into the footer. A long footer is as hard to scan as a long menu. Link to the events page and the sermon library instead of listing individual items that expire. When a link is retired, remove it in the same edit that unpublishes the page.

Review the paths twice a year

Twice a year, ask a person who does not edit the site to complete five tasks: find the Sunday time, find what you believe, find this month's main event, find a recent sermon, and find a human to email. Watch where they hesitate. Rename the label that confused them. Do not add a popup to compensate for a vague menu.

Write down the labels you chose and keep them stable. Changing visit to connect to experience every quarter trains nobody and breaks old links from emails. If you want help simplifying a menu that grew over a decade, describe the pages you have and which tasks guests fail. A shorter menu is usually the fix.

Frequently asked questions

Should Give be in the main menu?

Yes if people look for it every week, but it does not need the first position. Members who already give often use a bookmark or a footer link.

What about a search box?

Search helps on a large site. It does not replace clear labels. If search is weak or empty, fix the menu before you rely on it.
