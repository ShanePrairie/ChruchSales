Photography for Church Websites: A Simple, Respectful Guide

Website photos should be permitted, honest about who gathers, and light enough to load on a phone.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

Website photos should be permitted, honest about who gathers, and light enough to load on a phone.

Ask before you publish a face

A photograph on a public website is different from a snapshot in a group text. Before a face goes on the homepage, the person should know the photo may be seen by anyone, for as long as the page is up. A spoken yes for a printed directory is not automatically a yes for the open web. Give people an easy way to decline without missing the gathering, and honor a later request to take a photo down.

Write the practice in a short paragraph the office can repeat, and point photographers to it. You do not need a legal treatise on the website. You do need a habit. The accessibility checklist then asks for alt text that describes the scene rather than stuffing the picture with slogans.

  • Tell people when photos are being made for the website.
  • Offer a way to opt out before the gathering, not only after publication.
  • Remove a photo promptly when someone asks.
  • Do not tag private full names on a public page without a reason.

Treat children's images with extra care

Parents and guardians should decide whether a child appears on a public page. A group shot from across a room, where no child is the subject, is a different decision from a close portrait. Many churches simply do not feature identifiable children on public pages, and use photos of spaces, hands at a craft table from behind, or adults who have agreed. That restraint is easier to explain than a complicated exception.

The children's ministry page can describe check-in and safety without using a child's face as decoration. If you do publish a permitted photo, do not add the child's name, school, or neighborhood. Keep the original files somewhere the whole volunteer team cannot casually download and reshare.

Show the church you actually are

Stock photos of a different sanctuary, a different city, or a permanently young crowd teach guests to distrust the next sentence. If your room is small, photograph the small room in honest light. If the congregation is varied in age, do not illustrate the site with only one group. A simple phone photo of the real entrance helps more than a cinematic image of a building you do not occupy. The guest website checklist is the wider list this photo advice should sit beside.

Worship photos should respect the moment. Avoid hovering over someone who is upset or in prayer for the sake of a compelling frame. Stage a few planned pictures of the lobby, the children's check-in sign, the seats, and the outside door. Those images answer visit questions. A haze machine and a lens flare do not.

Prepare files for the page, not the gallery

Crop for the space the design uses, then export a file that is no larger than that space needs. A homepage hero does not need the original camera file. Name the file in a boring, descriptive way so the next editor knows what it is. The speed guide explains why oversized photos push the service time down the page.

Write alt text when you upload, while you still remember who is in the picture and whether they consented. Decorative crops can have empty alt text. Informational photos, such as the front door guests should use, should say that in the alt text and also in a visible caption if the fact matters. Do not put the only copy of a time or address inside the picture.

Keep a short library the team can trust

Save a small set of current, permitted photos in a folder with the date and the use you agreed to. Delete or archive images when a leader moves on, a room is renovated, or permission changes. Once a year, click through the public site and replace anything that no longer looks like Sunday. Outdated photos are a form of outdated information.

If you want help setting up a page that uses real photos without turning the site into a portfolio, describe the images you already have permission to use. A few accurate pictures, kept light and labeled, will outlast a shoot nobody can publish.

Frequently asked questions

Can we use a photo from the livestream?

Only if the people in it knew the service might be captured and published, and you still remove anyone who asks. A wide shot of the room is safer than a close crop of one face.

What if we have almost no photos yet?

Photograph the building, the entrance, and the empty room in daylight. Those facts help a guest more than a stock crowd that is not yours.
