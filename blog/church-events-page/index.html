Build a Church Events Page People Will Actually Use

An events page works when every listing says what, when, where, who it is for, and how to take part.

November 25, 2026By Modernized Ministry4 min read

An events page works when every listing says what, when, where, who it is for, and how to take part.

One page should answer whether to go

People do not want a poster dump. They want to know if an event is for them and whether they can get there. Each listing needs a name, a date, a start and end time, a place, a one-sentence description, who it is for, and what to do next. If registration is required, say so before the paragraph of background. If childcare is offered, say the ages. If it is not offered, say that too.

Link the events page from the main menu and from the homepage when something timely is coming. The navigation guide explains why that label should say events, not a brand name nobody recognizes. Social posts can point at one listing. They should not be the only place the date exists.

  • Name, date, start, and end.
  • Address or the room, written out.
  • Who is invited, in plain language.
  • Cost only if there is one, and what it covers.
  • How to sign up or simply show up.

Separate recurring gatherings from special dates

Sunday worship is not an event in the same sense as a Saturday service day. If every weekly meeting fills the calendar, guests cannot see the thing that is actually unusual this month. Give regular gatherings a standing page for times, and let the events page highlight dates that need a decision: a class, a meal, a parents' night, a service project.

For recurring items you do list, show the next date, not only every Wednesday. A person scanning on Thursday needs to know the next occurrence. Automated calendars can do this if someone owns the feed. A hand-built page is fine when the list is short and a named person updates it on the same day the bulletin is finished.

Remove what is over

An events page full of last month's dates teaches people to ignore it. Take down or archive a listing within a day after the event, unless you are intentionally leaving a short recap. Do not leave a register button that still accepts names. If a date moves, edit the listing rather than posting a second one and hoping readers compare them.

Put the update habit on a calendar the office already uses. The person who changes the bulletin can change the page in the same sitting. If that is unrealistic, keep fewer listings and keep them right. A short accurate page is more hospitable than a busy page nobody trusts. The outreach ideas guide is full of gatherings that only work if the details stay current.

Collect only what you need to host the event

A signup form should ask for the facts the team will use: name, the number of people coming, a way to reach them if plans change, and anything safety-related such as a child allergy for a meal. Do not add a long survey because the form tool allows it. Say how you will contact people and whether that includes text messages. If you want texts, ask for that permission separately.

For events inside homes, do not publish a private address on the public website. Offer a way to request the details after a person identifies themselves to the church. Public community events can name the park, the campus, or the hall. Match the tone of the listing to the invitation you would make in person. Hype does not compensate for a missing end time.

Connect the listing to the rest of the welcome

A guest who comes to a weeknight event may never have seen a Sunday service. The listing can include one sentence on what Sunday is like and a link to the plan-your-visit page, without turning the event into a bait-and-switch. After the event, follow up only with people who asked for it. The weekly content plan can feature one event, not a different event every day.

If the page is hard to maintain because the tools fight the team, describe the calendar you have and who updates it. The right fix might be a simpler page, not another widget. A listing a volunteer can edit on a phone between meetings will stay alive longer than a perfect layout nobody opens.

Frequently asked questions

Should we embed a third-party calendar?

Only if it shows the next date clearly on a phone and someone is responsible for it. A short list the office edits is better than a widget that hides the time.

How far ahead should events appear?

Far enough for people to plan, often a few weeks to a couple of months. Dates beyond that can wait unless registration truly has to open early.
